# VAWA Evidence Checklist 2025 — What USCIS Really Looks For

Filing a **VAWA self-petition (Form I-360)** means asking the U.S. government for protection from an abusive U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident family member. USCIS expects thoughtful, well-organized evidence — not just emotion. The stronger and clearer your documentation, the faster the case reviewer can decide your petition. USCIS will accept *any credible evidence*, but there are common documents that consistently help prove each required element. 

Below is a practical, client-facing checklist you can use to prepare a complete VAWA packet in 2025 — plus drafting tips, what to do when police reports are missing, and how to present sensitive evidence safely.

---

## Quick overview — What USCIS requires you to prove
You must show by a **preponderance of the evidence** that you meet all applicable elements for VAWA (relationship to the abuser, residence with abuser when required, battery or extreme cruelty, and good moral character). USCIS guidance emphasizes that *any credible evidence* may be used — but some documents carry more weight in practice. 

---

## Core evidence categories (and examples)

### 1) Identity documents (always include)
- Passport biographic page (copy)  
- Birth certificate (with certified translation if not English)  
- National ID / other government ID  
- Photo ID (driver’s license, state ID)  
**Why:** Proves who you are and matches your sworn declaration. Missing identity docs are an easy RFE trigger. 

---

### 2) Proof of qualifying relationship
(Which relationship depends on your basis — spouse, parent, or child.)
- **Spouse:** Marriage certificate, joint tax returns, joint lease/mortgage showing both names, wedding photos, affidavits discussing the relationship.  
- **Parent/Child:** Birth certificate showing the relationship, adoption or guardianship papers.  
- **Other evidence:** If a marriage certificate isn’t available, multiple corroborating documents and sworn statements are accepted. 

---

### 3) Proof of cohabitation / shared life (if required)
- Joint lease/mortgage or utility bills showing same address  
- Mail addressed to both at same residence, joint bank accounts, insurance policies showing spouse as beneficiary  
- School records for children showing same household address  
**Tip:** If you didn’t live together for long, explain circumstances clearly in your declaration and show alternative evidence of the relationship. 

---

### 4) Evidence of battery or extreme cruelty (the heart of the VAWA case)
USCIS accepts a wide range of proof; include everything relevant and available:

**Primary (strong) evidence**
- Police reports or incident reports (even if the case was not prosecuted)  
- Court records (protective orders, criminal filings, restraining orders)  
- Medical records documenting injuries, emergency room notes, clinic or counseling records  
- Photographs of injuries or property damage (with dates/affidavit explaining each photo)  
**Corroborating evidence**
- Affidavits/witness statements from friends, family, neighbors, clergy, co-workers, social workers, shelter staff — sworn and signed.  
- Records from shelters or domestic violence service providers.  
- Counseling notes, therapy records, or letters from mental-health professionals.  
- Phone records, threatening texts/emails, voicemail transcripts, social-media harassment screenshots (include metadata where possible).  
**If police reports are missing:** Detailed, contemporaneous personal declarations plus multiple independent affidavits, medical documents, shelter intake forms, and other third-party evidence can still meet the standard. Advocacy groups and specialized checklists confirm USCIS accepts *any credible evidence* when police reports aren’t available. 

---

### 5) Evidence of abuser’s U.S. status
USCIS must be able to verify the abuser is a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident:
- Copy of abuser’s U.S. passport, birth certificate, Certificate of Naturalization, or green card (front and back).  
- If unavailable, a public record or prior immigration filing (I-130 receipt, etc.) that shows status. 

---

### 6) Good moral character (GMC)
You must show you are of good moral character for the relevant period. Evidence includes:
- Police clearances / criminal background checks from the U.S. and from countries where you lived (if available)  
- Court dispositions for any arrests (showing outcomes)  
- Letters from employers, community leaders, clergy, and non-profits attesting to your character  
- Tax records, employment records, school enrollment — anything that shows stability and contribution to community  
**Note:** Full disclosure of arrests/charges is crucial; nondisclosure can be fatal to the case. 

---

## How to prepare sworn statements & affidavits (best practice)
- **Your personal declaration:** A clear, chronological narrative (dates, locations, what happened, who was present, injuries, how you escaped/searched help). Be factual and avoid exaggeration — explain things you don’t remember. Include why you didn’t report (fear, control by abuser, cultural or language barriers).  
- **Witness affidavits:** Short, first-hand recollections from people who saw abuse or its effects. Have witnesses state how they know you, where/when they observed abuse, and any documents they relied on. Use notarization when possible. 

---

## Organizing your packet — make it EASY for the adjudicator
- **Cover letter / index:** One page that lists everything inside and maps evidence to legal elements (relationship, residence, abuse, GMC).  
- **Tab or divider system:** Group documents under headers: Identity / Relationship / Cohabitation / Abuse / GMC / Translations.  
- **Label documents:** For each piece of evidence, include a short caption (e.g., “Photo A — bruising observed on 12/10/2024; see sister’s affidavit at Tab 6”).  
- **Translations:** Any non-English document needs a certified translation with a translator’s statement. USCIS rejects untranslated documents. 

---

## Safety & privacy considerations
- **Redact unnecessary personal data** that might put you at risk (only when safe to do so) — but keep originals in your private file.  
- **Use secure transmission** when sending sensitive records (encrypted email or in-person delivery to counsel).  
- **Discuss safety planning** with a domestic-violence advocate — many legal services and shelters help collect evidence confidentially. 

---

## What to do if you lack the “strong” documents (no police, no hospital)
USCIS guidance and practice manuals recognize many survivors never report to police. In those cases:
- Provide a **detailed personal declaration** explaining why (fear, language, lack of access, control).  
- Supply **multiple third-party affidavits** (friends, family, neighbors, co-workers, clergy, teachers, shelter staff).  
- Submit alternate documentary evidence: shelter intake forms, counseling records, immigration or school records showing sudden moves, photographs with explanatory affidavits, and contemporaneous notes. Agencies and checklists like NIWAP and ILRC provide examples of acceptable alternative evidence. 

---

## Common RFE triggers — and how to avoid them
- **Untranslated documents** → include certified translations.  
- **Weak or lonely affidavit** (only one affidavit with no corroboration) → add third-party statements and objective records.  
- **Missing proof of abuser’s status** → attach any available proof or explain why proof is unavailable.  
- **No proof of cohabitation** where required → supply utility bills, mail, or affidavits explaining living arrangements.  
- **Incomplete GMC evidence** → be thorough: include clear explanations and supporting documents for any prior issues.

If you receive an RFE, respond exactly to what is requested — do not over-submit irrelevant documents. If the RFE asks for a police report and you never reported, supply a sworn declaration explaining why plus alternative corroborating evidence. 

---

## Short checklist you can copy/paste
- [ ] Form **I-360** (completed & signed) — no filing fee for most VAWA self-petitions. 
- [ ] Personal declaration (detailed & dated)  
- [ ] Proof of identity (passport, birth certificate)  
- [ ] Proof of qualifying relationship (marriage, birth, adoption docs)  
- [ ] Proof of cohabitation (leases, utilities, mail)  
- [ ] Evidence of abuse: police reports, medical records, protective orders, photos, shelter records, text/phone records, affidavits  
- [ ] Evidence of abuser’s U.S. status (passport, green card, naturalization)  
- [ ] Good moral character evidence (police clearances, letters, tax/work records)  
- [ ] Certified translations for non-English documents  
- [ ] Cover letter + tabbed index (documents mapped to legal elements)

---

## Final tips (practical & safety-focused)
1. **Start early.** Collecting medical and third-party records can take time.  
2. **Organize for your reviewer.** A neat packet with a short cover letter reduces RFEs.  
3. **Be truthful and specific.** Dates, places, and names matter — be precise when possible.  
4. **Consult specialized counsel.** VAWA petitions are fact-sensitive; a short attorney review can prevent fatal mistakes.  
5. **Protect your safety.** If you fear retaliation, work with local victim-services organizations to collect and store evidence safely.
