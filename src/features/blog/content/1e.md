# Work Permits in the U.S.: Who Qualifies and How to Apply (2025 Guide)

Want to work legally in the United States but unsure where to start? Most non-citizens who are authorized to work get an **Employment Authorization Document (EAD)** — the plastic card (Form I-766) that proves you may work. This practical 2025 guide explains who commonly qualifies, the forms and timelines involved, how to apply (Form **I-765**), auto-extension rules, and concrete steps to reduce delays.

> **Quick snapshot:** To request work authorization you generally file **Form I-765**. Different immigration categories (asylum applicants, adjustment-of-status applicants, TPS, DACA, certain student work programs, and others) each have a specific EAD eligibility code and documentary requirements. 
---

## Key takeaways
- ✔️ **File Form I-765** to request an EAD — follow the category instructions carefully. 
- ✔️ **Asylum applicants** can apply for an EAD after their asylum application has been pending for the required waiting period (USCIS timing rules apply).   
- ✔️ **Pending adjustment of status** (I-485) applicants may request an EAD under category **(c)(9)** (often called a “combo card” when filed with travel permission). 
- ✔️ **TPS, DACA, and other humanitarian categories** are common EAD bases — check the specific form instructions and automatic-extension rules.  
- ✔️ **Automatic EAD extensions** exist for certain renewal categories — always check USCIS guidance to avoid a work-authorization gap. 

---

## 1. What is an EAD and why it matters
An **Employment Authorization Document (EAD)** is the standard evidence employers use to verify a non-citizen’s right to work in the U.S. — it’s a physical card (Form I-766) that lists your validity dates and eligibility code. Without an EAD, many non-citizens cannot be legally employed (unless another immigration status itself authorizes employment). USCIS manages EAD requests and issues the card once your Form I-765 is approved. 

---

## 2. Who commonly qualifies for an EAD? (Overview of main categories)

Below are the most searched and commonly used EAD bases — each has its own rules, forms, and documentary proof USCIS expects.

- **Asylum applicants (Category (c)(8))**  
  If you have a pending asylum application, you may file for an EAD after the required waiting period. There are filing rules and potential “EAD clock” pauses to watch for (see timelines below). 

- **Pending Adjustment of Status (Category (c)(9))**  
  Applicants who have filed Form I-485 to adjust status to lawful permanent resident commonly apply for an EAD while their green card case is pending. USCIS also issues “combo cards” that combine work and travel authorization. 

- **Temporary Protected Status (TPS)**  
  Nationals of designated TPS countries may request work authorization when they apply for TPS (Form I-821 + I-765 filings rules). TPS holders generally receive Employment Authorization tied to their TPS designation. 

- **DACA recipients**  
  DACA (Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals) grantees receive EADs under DACA rules; renewals and court orders have affected processing in recent years — check USCIS DACA guidance. 

- **Refugees and Asylees (a)(3)/(a)(5))**  
  Refugees receive employment authorization; those granted asylum (approved) are also eligible for EADs and may later adjust to lawful permanent resident status. 

- **Other categories** — certain nonimmigrant dependents (J-2, L-2), U- and T-visa applicants and derivatives, parolees (humanitarian parole), and other humanitarian bases may have EAD eligibility under specified codes — consult the Form I-765 instructions for exact codes. 

> **Note:** This list is not exhaustive; eligibility depends on your specific immigration classification and recent policy changes. Always check USCIS category guidance before filing.

## 3. How to apply — step-by-step (Form I-765)

1. **Identify your correct eligibility category** (Item 27 on Form I-765). The instructions list dozens of codes — this is critical. Filing under the wrong code will delay or reject your application.  
2. **Create a USCIS online account** (recommended) or prepare a paper Form I-765. USCIS now accepts many I-765 filings online — check the filing option for your category.  
3. **Gather supporting evidence**: copies of passport, I-94, prior EADs (if renewing), Form I-94/I-797 receipt notices, Form I-485 receipt (if c(9)), Form I-589 receipt for asylum-based c(8) filings, TPS receipts, DACA approval, etc. Follow the category checklist in the I-765 instructions. 
4. **Pay the fee or submit a fee waiver** (if eligible). Some categories (e.g., certain initial EADs for asylum applicants) may not require a fee — check the current I-765 instructions. 
5. **File**: online (preferred) or by mail to the correct lockbox address (USCIS provides direct filing addresses by category). Keep proof of filing (digital confirmation or postal tracking). 
6. **Attend biometrics** (if required). Some categories require fingerprinting; USCIS will send a biometrics notice if needed.  
7. **Wait for decision**. If approved, USCIS mails the EAD card to your address.

---

## 4. Timing: when you can apply and how long it takes

- **Asylum applicants:** you may **file an I-765 150 days after your properly filed Form I-589 is received**, but approval cannot happen until the application has been pending for 180 days in many circumstances — USCIS guidance and court rulings affect timing. Be mindful of “EAD clock” pauses if you or USCIS request delays. 

- **Adjustment applicants (c)(9):** timing depends on when you file I-485; many applicants file I-765 concurrently to avoid work gaps. USCIS optional checklists help ensure smoother processing. 

- **TPS / DACA / Humanitarian categories:** processing times vary by caseload and country designation; check USCIS processing times and TPS Federal Register notices for re-registration windows. Recent litigation and policy changes can affect whether new applications are accepted or renewals processed. 

- **General processing times:** USCIS posts processing-time estimates for Form I-765 by category/center — expect anything from several weeks to many months depending on the category and backlog. 
---

## 5. Auto-extensions, combo cards, and keeping your right to work

- **Automatic EAD extension:** For certain renewal categories, USCIS provides automatic employment-authorization extensions when you file a timely renewal and meet conditions — check the specific automatic-extension page and your eligibility code. This can prevent employment gaps while renewal adjudicates. 

- **Combo card (c(9)(P) / I-485 combo):** If you file for adjustment and request both work and travel permission, USCIS may issue a “combo card” that serves as an EAD and advance parole (travel) authorization — filing rules and fees differ. 

---

## 6. Practical checklist before you file (copy/paste this)

- ✅ Confirm your exact I-765 eligibility category (Item 27).   
- ✅ Create a USCIS online account and check whether your category supports online filing. 
- ✅ Collect identity documents (passport, I-94, prior EADs).  
- ✅ Gather category-specific evidence (I-589 receipt for asylum, I-485 receipt for adjustment, TPS filing proof, DACA approval notice, marriage/birth documents if needed).  
- ✅ Check the current fee or fee-waiver rules and prepare payment.  
- ✅ Keep digital and paper copies and note your receipt number.  
- ✅ If renewing, file before your EAD expires and verify whether you qualify for automatic extension. 

---

## 7. Common mistakes that cause delays or denials
- Filing under the wrong eligibility category (Item 27).  
- Submitting an I-765 before the required waiting period (common with asylum c(8) filings). 
- Missing or inconsistent supporting evidence (e.g., I-589 receipt, I-485 receipt).  
- Not filing renewals in time — create reminders well before expiration.  
- Using an outdated version of the form or sending to the wrong lockbox address. Always download the latest forms and filing addresses from USCIS. 
---

## 8. What to do if your application is delayed or denied
- **Check your USCIS account and receipt notice** for status and reasons.  
- **If delayed**, you can submit a case inquiry via USCIS online tools after the posted processing time has passed.  
- **If denied**, review the denial reason carefully — some denials can be appealed or you may be able to file a new, corrected application. Talk to an immigration attorney before filing appeals or new requests.

## 9. FAQs (short)
**Q: Can I start work as soon as I file Form I-765?**  
A: No. You must wait until USCIS approves your I-765 and you receive the EAD, except in narrow situations where your underlying status itself authorizes work. Filing alone does not authorize employment. 

**Q: How long does an EAD usually last?**  
A: EAD validity varies by category — commonly 1–2 years, but some categories (like TPS or DACA) follow period-specific dates. Always check the card’s dates and file timely for renewals. 

**Q: Are there categories with automatic work authorization (no EAD needed)?**  
A: Yes — certain statuses (for example, principal U or T nonimmigrants after approval, or some dependent statuses) may authorize work by virtue of status; check USCIS guidance for your specific case.

## 10. Final tips (to avoid common traps)
- Double-check the **eligibility code** and **filing address** before submitting. 
- Use USCIS online filing when available — it’s faster and gives better status tracking. 
- Keep copies of every document and proof of timely filing (mail tracking or online receipt).  
- If detained, or if you have criminal history or complex immigration history, consult an attorney before filing — mistakes can have serious consequences.
